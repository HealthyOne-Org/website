Healthiest and Unhealthiest Drinks: 10 Best, 8 Worst (2026)

November 26, 2026 · 8 min read · By Chris Hardaway
Photo via Pexels

Came here from TikTok? This is the longer version: every drink ranked, why it made the list, how much to have, and what to swap.

Most people think hard about what they eat and almost not at all about what they drink. But drinks are where a lot of sugar sneaks in without filling you up. A large sweet coffee, a bottle of juice and a soda with dinner can add up to more sugar than a dessert, and you will still be hungry at the next meal.

Below are the healthiest and unhealthiest drinks, ranked in plain terms: the 10 best to drink most days, the 8 worst to keep for rare treats, and the easy swaps in between. No detox teas, no cleanses, no "miracle" shots. Just what is in the glass.

How We Ranked These Drinks

A drink moves up the list when it does one or more of these:

  • Hydrates without much added sugar. Water is the baseline every other drink is compared to.
  • Brings something useful: protein, calcium, fiber, or plant compounds from tea, coffee or whole fruit.
  • Doesn't push you to drink more of it. Sugary drinks are easy to drink fast and rarely make you feel full.

A drink moves down the list when most of its calories come from added sugar or alcohol, when it hides a lot of sugar behind a "healthy" label, or when it packs more caffeine than most people need.

The 10 Healthiest Drinks

  1. Water (still or sparkling). Zero calories, zero sugar, and it does the main job of a drink better than anything else. Plain sparkling water counts. If plain bores you, add lemon, lime, cucumber, mint or a few crushed berries. Most adults do well aiming for water with every meal and between meals, and more when it is hot or you are active.
  2. Green tea. Almost no calories unsweetened, a gentle amount of caffeine, and plant compounds that tea is well known for. Hot or iced. Portion: 1 to 3 cups a day is a common, comfortable range.
  3. Black coffee. A plain cup has only a few calories. For most healthy adults, a few cups a day is fine; the general guideline is up to about 400 mg of caffeine a day, which is roughly 3 to 4 regular cups. Less if you are pregnant, sensitive to caffeine or have trouble sleeping. The problem is never the coffee; it is the syrup and whipped cream on top.
  4. Herbal tea. Peppermint, ginger, chamomile, rooibos and hibiscus are caffeine-free, so they are a good evening drink and an easy replacement for after-dinner dessert. Unsweetened, they have close to zero calories.
  5. Milk (dairy or soy). One cup of cow's milk has about 8 g of protein plus calcium. Unsweetened soy milk is the plant milk closest to that protein level. Portion: 1 cup. Skim or 1% if you want fewer calories; whole milk is fine if it fits your day.
  6. Kefir. A tangy, drinkable fermented milk with protein and live cultures. Buy plain and add your own fruit, because flavored kefir can carry a lot of added sugar. Portion: about 1 cup.
  7. A homemade protein smoothie. Blend 1 cup milk or unsweetened soy milk, 1/2 cup Greek yogurt, 1 cup frozen berries and a spoon of nut butter or chia seeds. That is roughly 20 g or more of protein with real fiber from whole fruit. This is a meal, not a side drink.
  8. Unsweetened plant milks (almond, oat, cashew). Fine for coffee and cereal, but most have very little protein compared with dairy or soy, and some oat milks have added sugar. Read the label for "unsweetened" and look for added calcium.
  9. Coconut water. Naturally a bit sweet with some potassium. Useful after a long, sweaty workout or on a hot day. It still has sugar, so it is not an all-day drink. Portion: 1 cup.
  10. Vegetable or tomato juice (low sodium). Much less sugar than fruit juice and gives you some vegetables. The regular kind can be very high in salt, so pick "low sodium." Portion: 1 small glass.

The 8 Unhealthiest Drinks

These aren't forbidden. They just shouldn't be your daily drink. Here is why each one lands at the bottom.

  1. Regular soda. A 12 oz can of cola has around 39 g of sugar, close to 10 teaspoons, and nothing else of value. Liquid sugar does not fill you up the way food does, so those calories mostly get added on top of what you eat. A 20 oz bottle is closer to 65 g.
  2. Energy drinks. Many combine a soda's worth of sugar with a large dose of caffeine. Big cans can make it easy to go well past a comfortable caffeine level, which can mean a racing heart, jitters and bad sleep. Not for kids or teens, and a bad idea late in the day.
  3. Sweet tea and lemonade. The tea or lemon is fine; the sugar isn't. A large restaurant or bottled sweet tea can match soda for sugar. Unsweetened iced tea with a squeeze of lemon gets you most of the way there.
  4. Specialty coffee drinks. Large flavored lattes, frappes and anything with "caramel," "mocha" or "cookie" in the name can carry more sugar than a can of soda, plus whipped cream. They are dessert in a cup. Treat them that way.
  5. Fruit "drinks" and juice cocktails. Words like "drink," "cocktail" or "beverage" on a juice label usually mean it is mostly water and added sugar, with a little juice. Check the ingredients; if sugar or corn syrup is near the top, skip it.
  6. Sports drinks (when you aren't sweating hard). A 20 oz bottle has roughly 30 to 35 g of sugar. They have a place in long, hard exercise or real heat. Sipped at a desk or with lunch, they are just a lighter soda.
  7. Sugary cocktails and mixed drinks. Margaritas, daiquiris, piña coladas and mixers made with syrup stack alcohol calories on top of sugar. Alcohol also tends to lower your guard around food later. If you drink, a simpler drink (a spirit with soda water and lime, a glass of wine) and a glass of water between rounds is a better pattern.
  8. Large glasses of fruit juice. Even 100% juice is the fruit's sugar with most of the fiber removed. An 8 oz glass of orange juice has about 20 g of natural sugar, and it is easy to pour twice that. Juice isn't poison; it is just a lot easier to overdo than an orange.

The Middle of the List: Fine With a Few Rules

  • 100% fruit juice: keep it to a small glass (about 4 to 6 oz) with a meal, or water it down half and half with sparkling water. Eating the fruit is better.
  • Kombucha: some brands are low in sugar, others aren't. Look for brands with less sugar per bottle and treat it as a once-a-day drink, not a health tonic.
  • Diet soda: no sugar, which makes it a step up from regular soda for many people. Water is still better. If diet soda helps you get off regular soda, that is a win; use it as a bridge to sparkling water.
  • Store-bought smoothies: many are mostly juice, frozen yogurt and sugar, with big portions. Ask for no added sugar and a smaller size, or make your own.
  • Drinkable yogurts and bottled protein shakes: read the label. Some are a good protein source; some are basically milkshakes. Look for real protein (15 g or more) and not much added sugar.

Simple Swaps That Make the Biggest Difference

You don't have to give up anything overnight. Change one drink a week and keep the ones that stick.

  • Soda → sparkling water with lime, or half soda and half sparkling water to start.
  • Large flavored latte → small latte with one pump of syrup, or a plain latte with cinnamon on top.
  • Sweet tea → half sweet, half unsweetened, then go lower each week.
  • Morning juice → a whole orange and a glass of water, or a 4 oz pour.
  • Energy drink → a cup of coffee or green tea, and a short walk outside for the afternoon slump.
  • Evening dessert drink → peppermint or chamomile tea.
  • Sports drink at your desk → water. Keep a full bottle where you can see it.

What to Skip

  • "Detox" teas, juice cleanses and "fat-burning" drinks. Your liver and kidneys already do the detox work. These drinks don't melt fat, and some just act as laxatives.
  • Labels that sell health on the front. "Natural," "made with real fruit" and "vitamin" on the front tell you nothing. The Nutrition Facts panel and the ingredient list tell you everything.
  • Drinking your hunger away. Replacing meals with sugary coffee drinks or juice leaves you low on protein and fiber. Eat the meal, drink water with it.
  • Mixing energy drinks and alcohol. The caffeine can hide how drunk you are.

How to Read a Drink Label in 10 Seconds

  1. Check servings per container. Many bottles are 2 servings. Double the numbers if you drink the whole thing.
  2. Look at "Added Sugars." Every 4 g is about one teaspoon. A drink with 40 g of added sugar is about 10 teaspoons.
  3. Look for protein. If a drink is going to have calories, protein is the best reason for them.
  4. Check caffeine on energy drinks and large coffees, especially after lunch.

FAQ

What is the healthiest drink besides water?

Unsweetened green tea, black coffee, herbal tea and plain milk or unsweetened soy milk are all strong picks. Tea and coffee add almost no calories, and milk adds protein and calcium.

What is the unhealthiest drink?

Regular soda and large energy drinks are near the bottom for most people: lots of added sugar, nothing that fills you up, and in energy drinks a big dose of caffeine. Sweet specialty coffees and sugary cocktails are close behind.

Is 100% fruit juice healthy?

It has vitamins, but it is the fruit's sugar without most of the fiber, so it is easy to overdo. Keep it to a small glass with a meal, or water it down. Whole fruit is the better choice.

Is diet soda better than regular soda?

It has no sugar, so for many people it is a step up from regular soda. Water and sparkling water are still better. Diet soda can work as a bridge while you cut back.

The Bottom Line

Make water, unsweetened tea, black coffee and milk your everyday drinks, and keep soda, energy drinks, sweet coffee drinks and big glasses of juice for now and then. Read the Added Sugars line (4 g is about a teaspoon), change one drink a week, and the swaps add up without giving up anything you love for good.
